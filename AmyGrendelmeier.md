# Between Dairy and Desire: Reading ‘Diary of a Wimpy Kid’ through the lens of Rene Girard’s Mimetic Theory

![WimpyKid1.png](jb-image:img_1795801258374_903922612bebb8)
The leftover in question: an abandoned slice of Swiss, rotting slowly in the corner of a middle school
basketball court. It’s wrapped in a delicate fuzz, spotted blue-grey, luminous and rancid in the midday
sun. A shadow looms over it, inching closer. This is Darren Walsh, soon to be the original victim of
the Cheese Touch. He peers down. It peers back. The court has been abandoned ever since the
cheese’s first appearance, but Darren still glances hesitantly from side to side. There are distant shouts
across the grass, far away enough that he feels brave enough to kneel down. He’s now face to face
with this dastardly leftover, close enough to see small beads of condensation clinging to it, caught in
its fur. Surely, just one touch wouldn’t hurt. His finger lightly skims its surface. He can feel the
velvety mould resting on damp skin. But something’s wrong. He freezes, surrounded by running
footsteps, and hears a shrill scream, “Darren’s touching the Cheese!” echoing across the field. And the
Cheese Touch hysteria ensues.

This is Kinney’s Diary of a Wimpy Kid, an illustrated epic detailing the tween experience of an
awkward, slouchy and prematurely balding Greg Heffley. And in his world, to have the Cheese Touch
is middle school pariahhood. Only one person at a time is infected, passing it on by touching a fellow
classmate. Greg tapes his fingers together to keep them crossed, the only way to protect himself from
transmission. The Cheese Touch is more than a game of tag. Victims remain ‘infected’ and socially
shunned for months before successfully being able to pass it on, if ever. In fact, every Cheese Touch
pandemic ends with a victim leaving the school.
Although details differ, the story feels familiar. We tend to gang up on and punish individuals who are
perceived as threats to social order, exemplified by modern cancel culture and right-wing populism.
René Girard, a French social philosopher and literary critic, was fascinated by this phenomenon of the
scapegoat. His interpretation of the Bible, Greek myths and literary classics led him to theorise the
existence of a hidden scapegoat mechanism that necessitates the periodic violent expulsion of an
individual to maintain harmony. Early in his academic career, he was inspired by Derrida’s essay La
Pharmacie de Platon. Derrida was interested in the ancient Greek ritual called ‘Pharmakos’, which
consisted of human ‘Pharmakoi’ being burned to death at times of city-wide crisis (such as famine or
pestilence). The city body was supposedly reunified through this act of violence; the Pharmakoi came
to represent a terrorising force whose destruction is necessary to restore order. Interestingly, these
Pharmakoi, or scapegoats, would be maintained at public expense for the purpose of their sacrifice.
Therefore, Pharmakos both depends on and contradicts the city boundary, deliberately creating an
‘Other’ for the sole purpose of its expulsion. Similarly, the Cheese Touch can only be transferred to
individuals within Greg’s middle school community- the outsider must come from within for the
scapegoat mechanism to function. Both rituals also use the language of disease to denote their victims,

extending a metaphor of physical contagion into the symbolic realm. Girard explains that ‘the victim
is considered a polluted object, whose living presence contaminates everything that comes in contact
with it and whose death purges the community of its ills’. Derrida thought that the contagion
metaphor exemplified his belief that violence was inherent to language itself; the Pharmakoi,
representative of both cure and disease, were burned to reestablish a purity that can never be realised
due to the innate ambiguity of meaning. Girard developed this by bringing it outside of the realm of
language. While he also connected violence with establishing a boundary between purity and
contagion, he thought that this could be derived from human nature itself.
Girard was interested in the fundamental compulsion to desire what others have or want. He thought
of desire as a triangular relation between three parties: a subject, a model and the desired object. The
subject’s desire is mediated by a model, whose own desire for the object makes it desirable. It’s
uncomfortable to be confronted with the idea that our desires do not spring from our relationship with
the desired object itself, but are rather always mediated through a third party. But it seems hard to
fully deny. As he explained in one of his 2007 Stanford lectures:
"We would like our desires to come from our deepest selves, our personal depths- but if it did, it
would not be desire.”
Inevitably, the subject and model begin to compete for the same object of desire. This escalates the
desire of both, leading to violence. Or rather, violence is this process of mimetic appropriation itself.
The desire for this object, and the mimetic rivalry that follows, continues to spread and infect a
community. Social order begins to splinter as individuals are pitted against each other. Individual
difference dissolves into a state of reciprocal conflict. So, what saves us?
In Diary of a Wimpy Kid, Greg Heffley longs for popularity. This is embodied by his model, the most
popular kid in school, Bryce Anderson. There is a rigid social ranking that each classmate falls into
(Heffley is 52nd by his calculation), all in competition for the top spot. This fight for status creates
jealousy, rivalry and tension that could easily boil over into violence, and sometimes does. But the
Cheese Touch represents a counter-mechanism capable of nullifying their mimetic rivalry. It
transforms the abstract fear of being a loser into the terror of a concrete individual. More importantly,
it unifies the school community as they come together to reject and expel the scapegoat. Greg notes
that once the Cheese Touch was gone, kids tried desperately to recreate it, supporting the Girardian

reading that the Cheese Touch exemplifies a systemic ritual of exclusion that is ingrained in human
society. Girard draws on the literature of Proust, Dostoevsky and Flaubert in his analyses, but Kinney
works just as nicely.
Girard’s thought was also heavily influenced by his religious beliefs. He thought that generative
violence (the destruction of a single victim) was able to be logically deduced from myths and rituals.
He compares his method of deduction to evolutionary theory; he uses fragments of evidence from
texts to piece together a hidden, structural reality. This structural compulsion toward scapegoating is
evident in the Cheese Touch. Its intangible mark and the arbitrary finger-crossing ritual is reliant on
social harmony and shared beliefs in a way akin to religion. The Cheese Touch relies on the tacit

consent of an entire collective group in order to function; they are drawn together by their mutual fear
of the polluted outsider. Girard notes that Biblical stories usually take the side of the scapegoat victim,
noting that this exposes the arbitrariness of the victim choice. The Bible has a distinct
‘counter-mythical thrust’ - by exposing the scapegoat mechanism, it becomes more easily
recognisable. Comparably, we also experience the Cheese Touch from a victim’s perspective. When
Greg claims that he threw the cheese away to save Rowley from embarrassment after he was made to
eat it, Greg is temporarily ostracised before he manages to pass it onto an unaware exchange student.
A potential criticism of mimetic theory is that by treating ideas and psychological states as the
primary drivers of reality, it ignores the concrete material conditions behind desire, conflict and
violence. For example, it doesn’t take into account Rowley’s utter indifference to the popularity
ranking. And when Greg voluntarily becomes the scapegoat to save his best friend’s reputation, it
would be interpreted as part of his desire to feel morally superior to his peers, rather than a purely
selfless act. His theory risks becoming unfalsifiable, reinterpreting every act in its own framework so
human motivation is reduced to mimetic desire and rivalry. Although it’s true that desire is usually
intensified by competition, I would say that this has no obvious connection to the expulsion of a
scapegoat. Desire isn’t always linked to rivalry, and Girard’s reasoning that society would fall apart
without this mechanism seems overstated. But I’ve still enjoyed his help in deciphering this odious
leftover. And his framework evokes a profound question: what if violence is intrinsic to social
harmony? Indeed, collective violence is often justified by its moral function: the preservation of social
order. But it seems hypocritical that society condemns violence while reserving its right to exercise it.
Perhaps the distinction between legitimate and illegitimate violence lies not in the nature of the acts
themselves but in the (sometimes cheesy) social structures that give them meaning.
